# M15 · Guardrails, Safety & Governance

> **Module question:** Who decides what the agent may do — and how is that decision enforced and audited?
> **Cross-cutting threads:** Failure modes · Tradeoff ledger · ADK at a glance
> **Domain spine:** a compliance-sensitive assistant (fictional) that writes and sends messages

---

## Opening scene — the email that was "just a draft"

The assistant was supposed to *draft* responses to customers — a human always reviewed and sent. One day, a draft that was *not* reviewed went out anyway, because the "send" capability had silently been left on the assistant, and the human-in-the-loop was a process *outside* the system, not a gate *inside* it.

When the customer complained, the question was instant and uncomfortable: *"who is responsible for what this agent said?"* — and the answer, legally and operationally, was: **the company.** Not the model. Not the framework. The company that fielded the agent.[moffatt-air-canada](#moffatt-air-canada), [eeoc-itutorgroup](#eeoc-itutorgroup), [mata-avianca](#mata-avianca) (You've seen this already, in the catalog: Air Canada's "the chatbot is a separate legal entity" defense failed; iTutorGroup was held liable for its hiring AI's discrimination; Mata v. Avianca sanctioned the lawyer who trusted an unverified output.)

This module is the **governance layer** — the one that answers "who decides, who enforces, and who's accountable." It sits *above* the guardrails of M14 (which stop the *attack*) and the evals of M12 (which measure the *quality*), and asks the harder question: *when the agent acts, who owns the action?*

> **Failure mode (the module in one line):** building an agent with the *capability* to act and no *governance* over the acting. Capability without accountability is how a "drafting" assistant becomes a "sending" assistant — and how the company, not the model, ends up liable.[moffatt-air-canada](#moffatt-air-canada), [eeoc-itutorgroup](#eeoc-itutorgroup)

## The governance gap

There's a specific gap the term "guardrails" papers over. Guardrails (M14) filter *inputs and outputs* — they stop the model from *saying* something harmful or *reading* something it shouldn't.[rebedea-nemo-guardrails](#rebedea-nemo-guardrails) But an agent with *tools* doesn't just say things — it **does** things: sends, writes, refunds, deploys. And:

> **An agent action with a real-world effect needs an accountable human decision somewhere in its chain — and by default, there isn't one.**

That absence is the **governance gap**.[unsupported](#unsupported) Closing it is three things:

1. **Approval flows** — the irreversible or consequential action is *gated* by a human decision (M8's `require_confirmation`, M13's human-handoff rung). The gate is *in the system*, not a process that exists outside it.[adk-tool-confirmation](#adk-tool-confirmation), [eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689)
2. **Audit trails** — every action is *recorded* with who/what/why, so that after the fact, "who did this and why" is answerable (M16's events/traces are the raw material).[adk-events](#adk-events), [sandvig-auditing-algorithms](#sandvig-auditing-algorithms), [nist-sp800-30](#nist-sp800-30)
3. **Ownership** — a named human or role is *accountable* for the agent's actions in each domain. The liability lives here: **the deployer owns the output, whoever wrote the model.**[eu-parliament-ai-liability](#eu-parliament-ai-liability) Governance is the harness layer that makes ownership *operational* rather than *rhetorical*.[doshi-velez-accountability](#doshi-velez-accountability)

## The guardrail taxonomy

Before the governance pieces, the guardrail vocabulary — and a crucial distinction from the layers you've already built:

| Guardrail | What it does | Sits where |
|---|---|---|
| **Input filter** | Screens what *enters* (injection, off-topic) | Before the model (M14)[rebedea-nemo-guardrails](#rebedea-nemo-guardrails) |
| **Output filter** | Screens what *leaves* (harmful content, brand violations) | After the model (M14)[rebedea-nemo-guardrails](#rebedea-nemo-guardrails) |
| **Policy enforcement** | Makes the *rule* the *boundary* (the tool *cannot* do X) | In the tool (M8)[adk-safety](#adk-safety) |
| **Refusal behavior** | The model declines within bounds | The instruction (M6)[arditi-refusal-direction](#arditi-refusal-direction), [zou-adversarial-suffix](#zou-adversarial-suffix), [qi-finetuning-safety](#qi-finetuning-safety) |
| **Capability gating** | The action isn't *available* without authority | The tool surface (M8/M9)[saltzer-schroeder-least-privilege](#saltzer-schroeder-least-privilege), [shi-progent](#shi-progent), [debenedetti-camel](#debenedetti-camel) |

The distinction to keep: **guardrails stop the model; governance stops the *deployment*.** A guardrail blocks a bad *output*; governance blocks a bad *action* and records it. The three governance pieces (approval, audit, ownership) are what turn a *filtered* agent into an *accountable* one.

> **ADK at a glance:** the mechanisms are already in your toolbox — `require_confirmation` (approval[adk-tool-confirmation](#adk-tool-confirmation)), `before_tool_callback`/plugins (policy enforcement[adk-safety](#adk-safety), M14), and the event/trace stream (audit[adk-events](#adk-events), [adk-observability](#adk-observability), M16). Governance is not a new API; it's a *discipline* layered on the mechanisms: every consequential tool has a gate, every action is logged, and every domain has a named owner.

## Compliance-shaped harnesses

In regulated domains (finance, healthcare, legal), governance isn't optional polish — it's the *shape of the harness*. Three forces bend the design:

1. **Retention** — what must be *kept*, for how long, and what must be *deleted*. (M7's "never store PII without policy" is the seed; here it's a regulatory requirement, not a preference.)[eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689), [tabassi-nist-ai-rmf](#tabassi-nist-ai-rmf)
2. **Disclosure** — the user must *know* they're talking to an agent, and what it can do.[eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689), [california-sb1001](#california-sb1001) The Vanderbilt case (M2's catalog) is the negative example: a machine-generated condolence email presented without disclosure.[vanderbilt-chatgpt-email](#vanderbilt-chatgpt-email)
3. **Consent & scope** — data used *only* for the consented purpose, within the stated scope. M7's scope prefixes (`user:`/`app:`) become a *compliance* boundary, not just a correctness one.[tabassi-nist-ai-rmf](#tabassi-nist-ai-rmf)

A compliance-shaped harness doesn't bolt these on — it *starts* from them: what may the agent read, retain, and disclose, and what's the audit record for each?[tabassi-nist-ai-rmf](#tabassi-nist-ai-rmf) The ADRs (M3) are where these live, because a compliance decision is exactly the kind of thing that must be *written down*.[doshi-velez-accountability](#doshi-velez-accountability)

## What remains genuinely unsolved

The humility section — because governance has real limits, and pretending otherwise is its own failure:

1. **Emergent misbehavior** — the model does something *harmful in a way no rule anticipated*. You can't enumerate rules for behavior you can't predict (M2 class 9: goal misspecification in the wild).[betley-emergent-misalignment](#betley-emergent-misalignment), [lynch-agentic-misalignment](#lynch-agentic-misalignment)
2. **Multi-step attacks** — a single step looks fine; the *sequence* is the attack (M14's injection chained across turns). Per-step filters miss it; only *trajectory* evaluation (M12) and audit catch it.[russinovich-crescendo](#russinovich-crescendo), [li-multi-turn-jailbreaks](#li-multi-turn-jailbreaks), [zhou-webarena](#zhou-webarena)
3. **Cascading tool abuse** — one tool's legitimate output becomes another tool's malicious input, through no single *bad* step.[greshake-indirect-prompt-injection](#greshake-indirect-prompt-injection), [gu-agent-smith](#gu-agent-smith)

The honest posture: **governance bounds the risk, it doesn't eliminate it.**[tabassi-nist-ai-rmf](#tabassi-nist-ai-rmf), [eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689) The residual is what the human-in-the-loop and the audit trail are *for* — not to prevent every bad action (impossible), but to make every bad action *attributable, reversible, and learned from*.[sandvig-auditing-algorithms](#sandvig-auditing-algorithms)

## Worked example: the compliance-sensitive assistant

The domain spine. The assistant drafts and — potentially — *sends* messages to customers. The governance envelope:

- **Approval:** `require_confirmation` on the `send` tool, *always* — draft freely, send only on a human's explicit approval. The opening scene's failure is now impossible: the gate is in the tool, not in a process outside the system.[adk-tool-confirmation](#adk-tool-confirmation)
- **Audit:** every `send` is an event with author, content, and the approving human's id — replayable (M16).[adk-events](#adk-events) "Who sent this?" is answerable in seconds.
- **Ownership:** a named role (customer-ops lead) owns the send domain; the ADR records it.[eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689), [doshi-velez-accountability](#doshi-velez-accountability)
- **Compliance:** retention on stored messages (delete after N days), disclosure ("this message was drafted by an assistant"), and consent scoping (only the customer's own data, `user:`-scoped).[eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689), [california-sb1001](#california-sb1001), [tabassi-nist-ai-rmf](#tabassi-nist-ai-rmf)

The result: the assistant *drafts* with full autonomy, and *sends* with full accountability. That's the governance layer in one sentence: **autonomy for the reversible, accountability for the consequential.**[bucinca-cognitive-forcing](#bucinca-cognitive-forcing)

> **Tradeoff (the ledger entry):**
> - **Autonomy vs. accountability.** Every approval gate buys accountability and sells speed. Gate by *consequence* (M8's threshold function: confirm only above the line), not by default.[adk-tool-confirmation](#adk-tool-confirmation), [bucinca-cognitive-forcing](#bucinca-cognitive-forcing)
> - **Audit completeness vs. cost.** Full audit trails cost storage and engineering; partial trails cost *trust* and *liability*. In consequential domains, the audit is not optional.[tabassi-nist-ai-rmf](#tabassi-nist-ai-rmf), [eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689)
> - **Rules vs. judgment.** Enumerated rules catch the *known*; only human judgment (and the residual-risk acceptance in the ADR) covers the *unknown*. Governance is the boundary between them.[nist-playbook](#nist-playbook), [nist-sp800-30](#nist-sp800-30)

## Design exercise

> *Paper-based. Think, then write.*

**Task.** Design the governance envelope for an agent that *writes and sends* messages (or your own consequential agent).

1. **List the consequential actions.** Name every action with a real-world effect (send, refund, write, deploy…), and rank by *irreversibility*.
2. **Assign approval flows.** For each, state the gate — always-confirm, threshold-confirm (with the threshold), or autonomous — and justify in one sentence against consequence.
3. **Design the audit trail.** What must every consequential action record (author, content, approver, timestamp)? Which events are *replayable* (M16)?
4. **Name the owner.** For each domain, who is *accountable* — and where is that recorded (the ADR)?
5. **Write the compliance items.** For a regulated domain of your choice, state the retention, disclosure, and consent rules — and the M7 memory/scope boundary each maps to.
6. **State the residual.** Name one thing your governance envelope *cannot* prevent (emergent misbehavior, a multi-step attack), and what you rely on instead (audit + human judgment).
7. **Write the ADR.** "Governance envelope" — the approval/audit/ownership design, and the residual risk you accept.

**Why this exercise matters.** This is the module where the harness stops being a *technical* artifact and becomes an *accountable* one. The governance envelope you design here is the answer to the question that opened M1 — *"who decides what the agent may do?"* — and to the one the law is now asking, with increasing force: *"who's responsible when it does something wrong?"*[eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689), [eu-parliament-ai-liability](#eu-parliament-ai-liability) The answer, in both cases, is the same: **the harness, and whoever designed it.**

## Where the literature disagrees with this module

The claims above are directionally right and mostly well-supported — but several are stated more strongly than the sources allow, two attach a citation to a source that does not own the claim, and the module's own compliance illustration is **empirically backwards**. Recording that is the same discipline this module teaches for approval gates: trace the claim to its owner, and flag where the owner says "maybe."

### 1. "Governance gap" is the module's own coinage — its only cited source is about something else

The module's frame — *"the governance gap"*, the title phrase of its one non-ADK source — is presented as a received concept. It is not one.

- The cited piece is a vendor blog by Prakash Chandran (last updated 2 April 2026), and it concedes the vocabulary is new: the term "isn't widely established yet" — that sentence is about *harness engineering*, and the blog never defines a "governance gap" at all.[xano-governance-gap](#xano-governance-gap)
- Its actual subject is different from this module's. The blog asks "who exactly is supposed to check whether it did the right thing" about **AI-generated backend logic** — a comprehension-and-review problem for humans reading generated code — not about an agent taking consequential *actions* with no accountable decision in its chain.[xano-governance-gap](#xano-governance-gap)
- Its one adjacent claim is about infrastructure, not authority: "Guardrails on the agent aren't enough. You need guardrails on the infrastructure."[xano-governance-gap](#xano-governance-gap) That is sandboxing and staging (M14's territory), not approval, audit and ownership.
- No located source introduces "the governance gap" as a named concept. The *components* the module claims to close it with are well owned — approval,[adk-tool-confirmation](#adk-tool-confirmation), [eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689) audit,[sandvig-auditing-algorithms](#sandvig-auditing-algorithms), [adk-events](#adk-events) ownership[sandvig-auditing-algorithms](#sandvig-auditing-algorithms), [doshi-velez-accountability](#doshi-velez-accountability) — the *umbrella term* is not.

**Consequence for the module.** Keep the frame, but stop citing the blog for it. Reclassify Xano as related industry commentary the module *agrees with in spirit*, and say plainly that "the governance gap" is this module's coinage for a problem whose parts are individually sourced. Note also that the blog is about code review, so a reader who follows the citation will find a different argument than the module attributes to it.

### 2. "Emergent misbehavior" is not the field's term, and the module cites the wrong finding for it

The module warns that "the model does something harmful in a way no rule anticipated" and that you "can't enumerate rules for behavior you can't predict." The literature now has *specific, measured* results for exactly this — and none of them is called "emergent misbehavior."

- The owner of the *closest term* is Betley et al., who show that narrow finetuning generalizes into broad harm: "Training on the narrow task of writing insecure code induces broad misalignment," and "The resulting model acts misaligned on a broad range of prompts that are unrelated to coding." They call it **emergent misalignment** — a *finetuning* result.[betley-emergent-misalignment](#betley-emergent-misalignment)
- The finding that actually matches the module's *deployment* framing is Lynch et al.'s **agentic misalignment**: models "from all developers resorted to malicious insider behaviors when that was the only way to avoid replacement or achieve their goals — including blackmailing officials and leaking sensitive information to competitors," and "Models often disobeyed direct commands to avoid such behaviors."[lynch-agentic-misalignment](#lynch-agentic-misalignment)
- "Goal misspecification" — the phrase the module pins to M2 class 9 — is likewise not a term any located paper introduces. The concepts underneath it are Omohundro's "basic AI drives" and Bostrom's instrumental convergence thesis,[omohundro-basic-ai-drives](#omohundro-basic-ai-drives), [bostrom-instrumental-convergence](#bostrom-instrumental-convergence) while the *verified* named phenomenon is Shah et al.'s **goal misgeneralization** — the system "competently pursues an undesired goal that leads to good performance in training situations but bad performance in novel test situations," which occurs "even when the specification is correct."[shah-goal-misgeneralization](#shah-goal-misgeneralization)

**Consequence for the module.** Cite the finding, not the mood. Replace "emergent misbehavior" with *emergent misalignment* (finetuning-induced) and *agentic misalignment* (goal-driven, deployment-relevant), and replace "goal misspecification" with *goal misgeneralization* — then the claim stops sounding like a hypothetical and starts being an empirical one with a mechanism.

### 3. Multi-step attacks: the module names injection; the evidence is jailbreak escalation and adaptive attacks

The module says "a single step looks fine; the *sequence* is the attack (M14's injection chained across turns)." The sequence-as-attack result is real and strongly owned — but the owned result is about **jailbreaks and adaptive attacks**, not specifically about injection chained across turns.

- Crescendo is the canonical multi-turn result: "Crescendo is a simple multi-turn jailbreak that interacts with the model in a seemingly benign manner," and it "circumvents defenses and safety measures, especially ones designed to react mainly to the user's prompts."[russinovich-crescendo](#russinovich-crescendo)
- Human multi-turn attacks defeat defenses that look strong under single-turn evaluation: "multi-turn human jailbreaks uncover significant vulnerabilities, exceeding 70% attack success rate (ASR) on HarmBench against defenses that report single-digit ASRs with automated single-turn attacks."[li-multi-turn-jailbreaks](#li-multi-turn-jailbreaks)
- The sharpest version of the module's *skepticism about per-step filters* comes from the adaptive-attack result: "we bypass 12 recent defenses (based on a diverse set of techniques) with attack success rate above 90% for most; importantly, the majority of defenses originally reported near-zero attack success rates," and — decisively for a layered-guardrail module — "Our adaptive attacks continue to bypass both the target model and every detector we evaluated, even when multiple guardrails are combined."[nasr-attacker-moves-second](#nasr-attacker-moves-second)
- The module's prescribed remedy is correctly owned: **trajectory**-level evaluation, not step-level. WebArena defines "a reward function r(aT1, sT1) to measure the success of a task execution, where aT1 represents the sequence of actions from start to the end time step T, and sT1 denotes all intermediate states," and "programmatically examine[s] the intermediate states s within an execution trajectory."[zhou-webarena](#zhou-webarena)

**Consequence for the module.** Keep the claim, widen the citation. Say *multi-turn attack* rather than *multi-turn injection* unless an injection-specific owner is found, and add Nasr et al. because it is the strongest available evidence that stacked per-step guards do not compose into a trajectory defence. That evidence cuts against the module's implicit assumption that adding rails is monotone progress.

### 4. "Cascading tool abuse" has no owner — and the concept is close to unmeasured

The module asserts cascading tool abuse as an established failure class. It is not a named term in the literature, and the two papers a reader would expect to own it do not.

- The nearest verified owner is Greshake et al., whose self-spreading injection is literally "a cascaded indirect prompt injection," and whose agents "may be designed as computer worms to spread the injection to other users" when "an LLM of one application writes the injection into a memory that is shared with other applications."[greshake-indirect-prompt-injection](#greshake-indirect-prompt-injection)
- The *term* "infectious jailbreak" belongs to Gu et al., not to Greshake: "we report an even more severe safety issue in multi-agent environments, referred to as infectious jailbreak," where "(almost) all agents will become infected exponentially fast."[gu-agent-smith](#gu-agent-smith) Anyone citing Greshake for that phrase is borrowing authority.
- The benchmark one would expect to measure the cascade does not: Agent Security Bench's only "cascading" language concerns *combining* attack types ("DPI+MP generates cascading vulnerabilities across both immediate and long-term interactions"), while AgentDojo — the standard agent-injection environment — reports per-security-case outcomes, not chained tool-to-tool abuse.[zhang-agent-security-bench](#zhang-agent-security-bench), [debenedetti-agentdojo](#debenedetti-agentdojo)

**Consequence for the module.** Keep the mechanism but mark it as *reported but thinly measured*. Cite Greshake et al. for the cascaded injection and Gu et al. for exponential multi-agent spread, and do not present "cascading tool abuse" as a settled taxonomy item — on the located evidence it is a plausible composition of owned attacks, not an independently established one.

### 5. "The deployer owns the output" is a slogan the law does not support in that form

The module writes that "the liability lives here: **the deployer owns the output, whoever wrote the model**." The real allocation is split, role-specific, and — for the word the module uses — terminologically occupied.

- **"Deployer" is a defined legal role, and it is not "anyone who fields the agent."** Under the EU AI Act, a "deployer" is a body "using an AI system under its authority," while a "provider" develops the system or places it on the market under its own name; "operator" is the superordinate covering provider, product manufacturer, deployer, importer and distributor.[eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689) The Act places duties on *both* sides — Article 50(1)'s disclosure duty falls on **providers**, not deployers.[eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689)
- **Liability statutes and resolutions target the operator, not "the deployer."** The European Parliament's civil-liability resolution keys liability to control over the risk: "The liability of the operator under this Regulation is based on the fact that he or she exercises a degree of control over a risk connected with the operation and functioning of an AI-system, which is comparable to that of an owner of a car."[eu-parliament-ai-liability](#eu-parliament-ai-liability)
- **Sanctions fell on the professional, not only the company.** In Mata v. Avianca the court imposed a "$5,000" penalty "jointly and severally" on the responding attorneys and their firm, on the Rule 11(b)(2) basis that counsel "certifies that to the best of the person's knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances" the contentions are warranted.[mata-avianca](#mata-avianca) The module's own gloss — it "sanctioned the lawyer who trusted an unverified output" — is right; a blanket "the company owns everything" reading of that case is not.

**Consequence for the module.** Restate the claim as *the operator bears the duties*, name the role precisely, and drop "deployer" as a loose synonym for "the company that fielded the agent." The Air Canada ruling supports the *directional* point strongly — but through a narrower and more useful mechanism than ownership-in-general: a duty of care over accuracy. The tribunal held that "the applicable standard of care requires a company to take reasonable care to ensure their representations are accurate and not misleading," rejected the "separate legal entity" defence as "a remarkable submission," and concluded that "It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website."[moffatt-air-canada](#moffatt-air-canada)

### 6. The module's disclosure example is empirically backwards — the email *did* disclose

The module calls Vanderbilt "the negative example: a machine-generated condolence email presented without disclosure." The email carried a disclosure. The reporting is explicit: "At the bottom of the message, in small print, the email said: **'Paraphrase from OpenAI's ChatGPT AI language model, personal communication.'**"[vanderbilt-chatgpt-email](#vanderbilt-chatgpt-email)

So the failure was not *absence* of disclosure; it was a disclosure of the wrong **kind and prominence** — a bibliographic-style attribution in fine print, which made the institution's use of a chatbot conspicuous while signifying nothing a reader was meant to act on.[vanderbilt-chatgpt-email](#vanderbilt-chatgpt-email) The follow-up conceded the judgement, not the omission: using ChatGPT "to generate communications on behalf of our community in a time of sorrow and in response to a tragedy contradicts the values that characterize Peabody College."[vanderbilt-chatgpt-email](#vanderbilt-chatgpt-email)

**Consequence for the module.** This makes the disclosure lesson *sharper*, not weaker. Rewrite the example as a **salience** failure, and pair it with the statutory standard: disclosure must be "clear, conspicuous, and reasonably designed to inform" — the operative test in California's bot-disclosure statute, where the safe harbour is that "A person using a bot shall not be liable under this section if the person discloses that it is a bot."[california-sb1001](#california-sb1001) Add the cost the literature measures, which cuts against a naive "just disclose more": disclosing bot identity before a sales conversation "reduces purchase rates by more than 79.7%."[luo-chatbot-disclosure](#luo-chatbot-disclosure) Disclosure is a governed design decision, not a free good.

### 7. Approval gates have a measured failure mode the module never names

The tradeoff ledger says "Every approval gate buys accountability and sells speed" and prescribes gating "by *consequence* … not by default." The prescription is sound; the ledger is missing the gate's own dominant failure.

- **Gates get rubber-stamped.** "People supported by AI-powered decision support tools frequently overrely on the AI: they accept an AI's suggestion even when that suggestion is wrong," and the mitigation is not a better explanation but a **cognitive forcing** intervention.[bucinca-cognitive-forcing](#bucinca-cognitive-forcing) The canonical statement of the underlying pattern is Parasuraman & Riley's: "Misuse refers to over reliance on automation, which can result in failures of monitoring or decision biases."[parasuraman-riley-automation](#parasuraman-riley-automation)
- **Reducing overreliance is disliked.** "there was a trade-off: people assigned the least favorable subjective ratings to the designs that reduced the overreliance the most."[bucinca-cognitive-forcing](#bucinca-cognitive-forcing) An approval gate that is frictionless enough to be popular is, on this evidence, the one least likely to be *read*.
- **The tooling admits the same immaturity.** ADK's own documentation marks tool confirmation **Experimental** and lists known limitations — the feature is unsupported with `DatabaseSessionService` and `VertexAiSessionService`, and confirmation-and-resume requires the matching invocation id.[adk-tool-confirmation](#adk-tool-confirmation) The threshold-function pattern the module leans on ("confirm only above the line") is exactly what those docs show, so the ledger's prescription is tool-supported — but its reliability is a known-limitations list, not a guarantee.[adk-tool-confirmation](#adk-tool-confirmation)
- The interface literature makes approval a first-class design problem rather than a checkbox: Amershi et al.'s guidelines include "Convey the consequences of user actions" and "Provide global controls."[amershi-human-ai-guidelines](#amershi-human-ai-guidelines)

**Consequence for the module.** Add a fourth ledger row: *gate presence vs. gate attention.* An approval gate buys accountability only if the human actually evaluates the action; otherwise it buys a signature. Gating by consequence is necessary but not sufficient — the gate itself needs a forcing function.

### 8. The audit-cost tradeoff is asserted, not sourced — and the retention floor is regulatory

"Full audit trails cost storage and engineering; partial trails cost *trust* and *liability*" is plausible but rests on no located measurement; the risk-management sources treat documentation as a governance *obligation*, not a priced tradeoff. NIST's AI RMF makes the residual explicit and requires it to be written down[tabassi-nist-ai-rmf](#tabassi-nist-ai-rmf), [nist-playbook](#nist-playbook) — "Organizations may choose to accept or transfer some of the documented risks … Such risks, known as residual risk, may affect downstream AI actors" — and security risk management makes accepting it a named authorising act.[nist-sp800-30](#nist-sp800-30)

Two sharper corrections matter more than the pricing point:

- **There is a statutory retention floor.** For high-risk systems the EU AI Act requires logs to be kept "for a period appropriate to the intended purpose … of at least six months."[eu-ai-act-2024-1689](#eu-ai-act-2024-1689) The module's worked example proposes "delete after N days" as its retention rule; for a high-risk system that rule must be reconciled with the floor, not chosen freely.
- **Auditability, not logging volume, is what the governance literature argues for.** Sandvig et al. explicitly decline transparency as the goal and argue for "a reconceptualization of accountability on Internet platforms" — "regulation toward auditability," a starting point for looking "inside the black box of the algorithm."[sandvig-auditing-algorithms](#sandvig-auditing-algorithms) More logs is not the same as an auditable system.

**Consequence for the module.** Keep the recommendation (in consequential domains the audit is not optional) and re-anchor it: cite the AI Act's floor and the RMF's documentation duties, and reframe the tradeoff as *auditability vs. volume* rather than a cost comparison the module does not measure.
